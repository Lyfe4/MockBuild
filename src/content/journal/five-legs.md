The stag beetle went up on the wall for a fortnight this autumn with five legs,
and nobody noticed. That is the part worth writing down.

Only the right half of an animal is drawn here; the left is the right one
reflected, which is both what a beetle is and the reason a plate cannot come out
lopsided. The consequence is that a single wrong sign is invisible. A middle
femur measured at minus its true distance from the midline lands on the left,
the reflection puts a second copy of it back on the left, and
[_Lucanus cervus_](/specimen/lucanus-cervus) stands there with three legs on one
side, two on the other and a bare patch where the sixth should be. The drawing
does not break. It looks almost right, which is worse.

It was found by a check rather than by an eye — a rule that every measured point
must sit at or right of the midline, run over every plate in the collection. The
check now names eight classes of mistake and all eight are of that kind: things
that produce a plausible drawing rather than a broken one.

The mandibles, meanwhile, are the reason this animal is accessioned first. They
are the identification, they are the drawing's whole character, and they were
right on the first pass.
